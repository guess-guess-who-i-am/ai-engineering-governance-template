# Research Evidence Method

## Routing instruction

Apply every rule below when this Skill is selected. These are the user's original rules translated into English, not a rewritten summary.

## User's original wording — English translation

- Before undertaking any task related to papers, first search for some award-winning papers and how Nature, Science, and Cell have accomplished this, learn from them, examine in detail exactly how they did it and why they are good, and only after completing the analysis and obtaining the methodology continue with the current task.
- When dealing with content related to papers, carefully check the following: the citation counts of its baseline and benchmark, whether it is CCF-A, preferably whether it is a best paper or oral presentation, etc., and preferably whether it is from within the past 2 years; preferably include all aspects from every angle. All of them should concern the same topic as the current one.
- Do not bolster others' morale while undermining our own; for all our claims and whatever else, if they do not quite align with the current content, strive to find supporting material for us, broaden our proof and make it more impressive, rather than merely shrinking it or being conservative. Be conservative only when there is no alternative and conservatism is unavoidable.
- We usually do not have a human review stage; if an authority requires expert review, we should, as much as possible, use other authoritative means, such as finding other materials or using other reliable methods to solve the problem, rather than requiring human review or expert review, etc.
- For the problems we need to solve, carefully examine how papers in Nature and top CCF-A conferences actually solve these problems; when encountering a problem that is not easy to solve, first search for at least two related papers; if it is very difficult, search for at most 10 papers; see how authoritative methods solve it and whether we can learn from them to solve it.
- When initially running experiments, the baselines used should include 10 highly cited ones (preferably with citation counts exceeding 100). Ideally, they should cover different aspects and represent different perspectives. We can look at papers close to ours to see what baselines they use. Apart from the pioneering foundational works, the others should preferably carry distinctions such as Spotlight or Oral at CCF-A top conferences.
- When initially running experiments, small-scale validation is acceptable; for example, select 10 to 20 examples from authoritative, highly cited benchmarks and run them. Being able to validate is sufficient.
- When finally writing the paper, run approximately 6 benchmarks as well. They should preferably cover different aspects, have high citation counts, and not consume excessive space; for example, the storage space for the entries to be run should be under 3G. We should also look at how many entries other papers run in their experiments; for example, if they run 200 entries per dataset, we should run 400.
- Never boost others' morale while undermining our own; apart from the related work section, the paper must not contain any wording that disparages or indirectly disparages us.
- I feel this is very much like “boosting others' morale while undermining our own”; it immediately lays out our own disadvantages. I feel this should not appear in writing, but it can appear during R&D: In external-facing writing, the body of the paper, and solution briefings, first state our problems, methods, results, and evidence; do not proactively lead with self-undermining content such as our disadvantages, risks, or statements that we cannot replace someone. Only limitations that would change the conclusion should be used to explain boundaries in a neutral, specific manner. R&D, debugging, and internal decision-making, by contrast, must directly document and analyze disadvantages, failures, risks, and unvalidated assumptions; facts that would change the conclusion must not be altered or concealed to preserve appearances.
