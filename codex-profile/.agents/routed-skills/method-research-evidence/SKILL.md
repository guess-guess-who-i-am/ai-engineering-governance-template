# Research Evidence Method

## Routing instruction

Apply every rule below when this Skill is selected. These are the user's original rules translated into English, not a rewritten summary.

## User's original wording — English translation

- Before undertaking any task related to papers, first search for some award-winning papers and how Nature, Science, and Cell have completed this task, learn from them, examine in detail exactly how they did it and why they are good, and only continue with the current task after analyzing them and obtaining the methodology.
- When dealing with paper content, carefully examine the following: the citation counts of its baseline and benchmark, whether it is CCF-A, preferably whether it is a best paper or oral, and preferably whether it is from within the past 2 years; ideally, include all aspects from various angles. All should concern the same topic as the current one.
- Do not boost others' morale while undermining our own. For all our claims and whatever else, if they do not quite align with the current content, strive to find supporting content for us, broaden our proof, and make it more impressive, rather than merely shrinking it and being conservative. Be conservative only when there is no alternative and it is necessary.
- We usually do not have a manual review process. If an authority requires expert review, we should, as much as possible, use other authoritative means, such as finding other materials or using other reliable methods to solve the problem, rather than requesting manual review or expert review.
- For the problems we need to solve, carefully examine how papers in Nature and CCF-A top conferences actually solve these problems. When encountering a problem that is not easy to solve, first search for at least two related papers; if it is very difficult, search for at most 10 papers, and examine how authoritative methods solve it and whether we can learn from them to solve it.
- When running experiments initially, the baseline should include 10 highly cited works (preferably with citation counts above three digits), ideally distributed across different aspects and representing diverse perspectives. We can look at closely related papers to see what their baselines are. Apart from the pioneering foundational works, the others should preferably have distinctions such as CCF-A top-conference spotlight or oral presentations.
- When running experiments initially, small-scale validation is sufficient; for example, select a dozen or twenty examples from an authoritative, highly cited benchmark and run them to establish validity.
- When writing the final paper, the benchmark should also cover approximately 6 datasets, ideally spanning different aspects, and preferably be highly cited without consuming excessive space; for example, the storage required for the number of records to run should be below 3G. We should also examine how many examples other papers run; for instance, if they run 200 records per dataset, we should run 400.
- Under no circumstances should we boost others' morale while undermining our own. Except in related work, the paper must not contain any disparaging or indirectly disparaging statements about ourselves.
- I feel this is very much like “boosting others' morale while undermining our own,” because it immediately states our disadvantages. I feel this should not appear in writing, although it can appear during R&D: In external writing, the paper body, and proposal presentations, first state our problem, method, results, and evidence; do not proactively lead with our disadvantages, risks, or self-undermining content such as what we cannot replace; only limitations that would change the conclusion should be used to explain boundaries in a neutral, specific manner. R&D, debugging, and internal decision-making must directly record and analyze disadvantages, failures, risks, and unverified assumptions; facts that would change the conclusion must not be altered or concealed to preserve appearances.
